How it works

Open a song and it's fed through a separation network. In Full mode that's HT-Demucs, Meta's network, which pulls out drums, bass, guitar, keys, vocals and everything else. In Quick mode it's a much smaller MDX-Net model that finds the vocals and leaves everything else as one instrumental, several times faster. Either way the vocals can then go through a third model, trained for karaoke, which lifts the lead singer off the backing vocals. Everything runs on your own computer; no audio ever leaves it.

Quick, Full or HD?

Pick on the drop card, or in Settings. Quick when you need a vocal, an instrumental, or a lead-vocal-free mix in a hurry. Full when you want the band taken apart. HD gives the same six instruments through BS-RoFormer, the network at the top of the open separation leaderboards: noticeably less bleed between stems, at several times the processor time. With the graphics card switched on it is a matter of minutes. A song keeps its Full and HD splits side by side, and the library opens the best it has.

What to expect

The first run downloads the models (about 136 MB for Full, 53 MB for Quick, 53 MB for the vocal split; once each). Full separation takes a few minutes per song on the processor, Quick a fraction of that — a progress bar shows where it's at, and the song plays while it works. A song you've split before opens instantly from the library.

Getting just one part, or everything but one

Every stem has a switch. Turn off the ones you don't want and export as one mix to get, say, an instrumental with no lead vocal. Solo a stem and export to get it alone. Or export the selected stems as separate files for your DAW.

When the split isn't clean

Separation is a best guess, not a master recording. Heavily layered vocals, distorted guitars and dense synths can bleed between stems — that's the state of the art, not a fault. Pads, organs and synths often land in Aux rather than Keys.
